Most budgets die in the second month. Not because people are undisciplined, but because a 40-category budget demands dozens of small decisions every week, and decision fatigue is real. The fix is to make tracking coarser, not finer. You don't need to know where every dollar went. You need to know **where the leak is**.


## Three categories, nothing else

1. **Fixed** — rent/mortgage, insurance, subscriptions, minimum debt payments. These barely change, so you record them once and move on.
1. **Flexible** — food, transport, shopping, entertainment. This is the only category that needs ongoing tracking, because it's the only one with real slack in it.
1. **Irregular** — anything over roughly 5% of monthly income in a single hit: car repairs, medical bills, gifts, travel. Track these separately because they're the ones that masquerade as \"unexpected\" when they're actually just *unscheduled*.


## After 30 days, answer three questions

- **What fraction of income goes to fixed costs?** Above ~50% and your problem isn't lattes — it's housing or transport, and only a big change (or a raise) fixes it. Knowing this changes which advice applies to you.
- **What are the top three lines inside flexible spending?** There are almost always one or two lines you can't reconstruct from memory. That's the leak. It's rarely the thing you feel guilty about.


## Practical notes

- Any tool works — a spreadsheet, an app, a note on your phone. The only test: **can you log an expense in under 30 seconds?** If not, you'll stop.
- Bank/credit card auto-categorization is fine, but strip out internal transfers and payments between your own accounts, or your surplus will be wrong every single month.
- The tracking phase is temporary. Once you can predict your surplus within ~10% without checking, stop tracking and go back to a quarterly check-in. Tracking is a diagnostic, not a lifestyle.

> A budget you abandon in week six tells you nothing. A month of coarse data you actually collected tells you everything you need next.
