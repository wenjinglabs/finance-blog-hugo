If you have multiple debts, the advice industry offers two famous orders. **Avalanche**: pay minimums on everything, throw all extra cash at the highest interest rate first. **Snowball**: same idea, but attack the *smallest balance* first, regardless of rate. Avalanche is mathematically optimal. Snowball is psychologically sticky — quick wins keep people going. Both claims are true, but they're usually stated without numbers. So I ran both, on the same debts, with the same budget.


## The setup

| Debt | Balance | APR | Minimum |
|---|---|---|---|
| Store card | $4,000 | 28% | $100/mo |
| Credit card | $8,000 | 22% | $160/mo |
| Personal loan | $3,000 | 8% | $90/mo |

Total budget for all debt payments: **$600/month**. Minimums add up to $350, leaving $250/month of attack money — the only variable between the two strategies is the *order* it lands in.

## The results, month by month

| Strategy | Order of attack | Debt-free | Total interest | First payoff |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Avalanche | 28% → 22% → 8% | 33 months | $4,489 | Month 14 |
| Snowball | \k → \k → \k | 35 months | $5,412 | Month 10 |

The gap: snowball costs **$923 more and takes 2 months longer** — but delivers its first complete payoff **4 months sooner**. That's the whole trade in three numbers. About 2.9% of total debt, in exchange for an early demonstration that the plan works.


## Where the difference comes from

Snowball pays off the 8% personal loan first — the *cheapest* money you owe — while the 28% store card keeps compounding against you for extra months. At 28% APR, that balance accrues roughly $93 of interest **per month** on its own. Every month of delay there is what the $923 is made of. The smaller the rate spread between your debts, the smaller this penalty shrinks: with debts at 19% and 22%, the two methods nearly converge.


## The part the math misses

A plan you abandon at month 9 loses to any plan you finish. Debt payoff fails for behavioral reasons far more often than mathematical ones, and an empty-feeling first year is precisely when people quit. Snowball's month-10 payoff — one fewer account, one less minimum payment, visible proof of motion — is buying compliance, not returns. Research on real borrowers — Gal & McShane’s [2012 study in the Journal of Marketing Research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmr.11.0272) — found that people who focus on one account at a time are more likely to eliminate debt entirely, regardless of order.


## The decision rule I'd actually use

1. **Rate spread is small (under ~5 points) or you're disciplined by nature → avalanche.** The penalty is trivial and the math is clean.
1. **Spread is wide but the small debt dies fast (within a few months) → snowball anyway.** Killing the loan in month 3 costs little and buys a lot of momentum.
1. **You have a 25%+ APR debt that is ALSO your biggest balance → hybrid.** Attack it first even if it's the largest; at that rate, it's both the math move and, eventually, the momentum move.

> The best debt payoff order is the one that's still running in month nine. Pick the version of yourself you actually are, and let the $923 be what it is: the price of a plan you'll finish.
