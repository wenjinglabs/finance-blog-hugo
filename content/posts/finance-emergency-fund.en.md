The emergency fund gets less airtime than any other financial topic because it's boring: no returns to brag about, no strategy to debate. But it does a specific job no other money can do — **it converts emergencies from financial events into inconveniences**. Every dollar of it exists so you never have to sell investments at the worst moment or put a crisis on a credit card.


## How much: do the arithmetic, not the folklore

The baseline is 3-6 months of expenses, but which end of that range you need is determined by three variables:

- **Income volatility.** Salaried with a stable employer: 3 months. Freelance, commission-based, or single-income household: 6+ months.
- **Number of earning adults in the household.** Two independent incomes are themselves a hedge; one income supporting several people needs the bigger buffer.
- **What a gap actually costs you.** Estimate honestly: if you lost your income today, how many months until a new one, at your field's real pace? Use that number, not optimism.

Note the base is **expenses**, not income. If your essential monthly spend is $3,200 and you settled on 5 months, the target is $16,000. Sizing off income inflates the target and makes people quit halfway.

What doesn't qualify, no matter how good it looks: anything with a lock-up period, anything whose value fluctuates, and anything with a withdrawal penalty. Chasing an extra 0.5% on $15,000 earns you $75/year. One forced sale of investments in a downturn routinely costs thousands. **The emergency fund is not where you optimize returns — that is a job for [index funds](/posts/finance-index-dca).**


## Three failure modes

1. **Investing the emergency fund** \"temporarily.\" The market drops exactly when jobs disappear — the two risks are correlated. This is the classic first-loss story.
1. **Counting a credit line as the fund.** Credit solves timing problems, not solvency problems, and it's repriced or cancelled at precisely the wrong moments.
1. **Never re-sizing.** Life changes — a kid, a mortgage, a move. Check the number against reality once a year.

> The emergency fund isn't an investment. It's the insurance policy that makes it rational to be an aggressive long-term investor with everything else.
