Every finance article recites the same example: $10,000 at 8% becomes $100,000 in 30 years — run it yourself on the [SEC compound interest calculator](https://www.investor.gov/financial-tools-calculators/calculators/compound-interest-calculator). True, and useless — because most people never see anything like it, for two reasons that live outside the formula.


## Detail one: compounding has a rear-loaded engine

Look at where the growth actually happens for that $10,000 at 8%:

- After 10 years: ~$21,600 — the first decade added about $11,600
- After 20 years: ~$46,600 — the second decade added about $25,000
- After 30 years: ~$100,600 — **the final decade added about $54,000, more than half the entire result**

This is why interruption is so expensive. Every withdrawal, every "I'll restart next year," every reset to chase a better fund puts you back on the flat part of the curve. People don't fail at compounding because they picked the wrong asset. They fail because **the average holding period is a fraction of the period the math requires**. This is also why the [emergency fund](/posts/finance-emergency-fund) comes before investing: it's the thing that stops life's surprises from interrupting the curve.


## Detail two: nominal returns are not buying power

An 8% nominal return during 3% inflation is roughly a 5% real return. Over 30 years that difference is enormous: $10,000 nominally becomes $100,600, but its *purchasing power* is only about $41,000 in today's money. And the flip side is worse: a typical checking account at 0.2% isn't "safe" — at 3% inflation it's a guaranteed real loss of about 2.8% per year. Money that looks intact is quietly shrinking.

Two practical consequences:

- **Always subtract inflation mentally.** Any product promising 4-5% \"guaranteed\" during normal inflation is offering you roughly zero real growth, minus fees — possibly negative.
- **Start earlier beats starting bigger.** Because the curve is rear-loaded, ten extra years at the beginning are worth more than double the principal — see the table above: the last decade outweighs the first two combined.


## The honest summary

> Compounding is not a trick that makes money grow. It's a promise that only pays out in full to people who can hold a position for decades and stay out of its way. Almost everything about good personal finance — the emergency fund, the allocation, the automation — exists to make you that person.
