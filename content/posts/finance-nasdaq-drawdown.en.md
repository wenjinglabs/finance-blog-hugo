Late October 2026: the Nasdaq dropped [2.6% in a single session](https://www.reuters.com/business/nasdaq-futures-drop-over-1-ai-worries-resurface-oil-climbs-2026-10-22) as doubts about AI spending resurfaced, led by semiconductors. Headlines explain *why* (AI capex skepticism, inflation worries, bond volatility, oil). This post is about the questions headlines don't answer: how bad can this get, how long do these things last, and — if you hold index funds — what's actually worth doing right now.


## First, the asymmetry everyone forgets

Losses and gains are not symmetric. The deeper the hole, the steeper the climb out:

| Drawdown | Gain needed to recover |
|---|---|
| -20% | +25% |
| -30% | +43% |
| -50% | +100% |
| -78% (dot-com crash) | +355% |

This is why "I'll sell now and buy back lower" is harder than it sounds: you need the market to fall far enough to cover the recovery math *and* you need to actually pull the buy trigger, which feels worst exactly at the bottom.


## Every major Nasdaq drawdown since 2000

| Episode | Peak → bottom | Depth | Time to new high |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dot-com crash | Mar 2000 → Oct 2002 | -78% | ~15 years (2015) |
| Financial crisis | Oct 2007 → Mar 2009 | -54% | ~7 years (2014) |
| Q4 2018 selloff | Aug 2018 → Dec 2018 | -23% | ~5 months (May 2019) |
| COVID crash | Feb 2020 → Mar 2020 | -28% | ~4 months (Jun 2020) |
| 2022 rate-cycle bear | Nov 2021 → Dec 2022 | -33% | ~2.5 years (Apr 2024) |

The dot-com row deserves its asterisk: deepest *and* longest. The 2018 row deserves the opposite one — at the time it felt like the beginning of the end, and it lasted five months.

Two honest takeaways. First: drawdowns of -20% to -35% are **routine** for a tech-heavy index — five in 26 years. If you hold Nasdaq-weighted funds, this is the admission price, not an anomaly. Second: recovery times ranged from 4 months to 15 years. Nobody knows which one any current decline turns out to be — which is exactly why the response shouldn't depend on guessing it.


## The three moves with math behind them

1. **Don't stop the automatic buys.** Fixed-dollar buying purchases more shares at lower prices — at -30%, the same $500 buys ~43% more shares than at the peak. DCA's edge exists *only* if it runs through the drawdown. Pausing during red months quietly deletes the entire mechanism and converts you back into a market-timer.
1. **Check the emergency fund, not the portfolio.** The real risk in a crash isn't the drawdown — it's being *forced* to sell into it. 3-6 months of expenses in cash means a job loss during the downturn doesn't touch the portfolio. If that buffer is intact, a -30% line on a screen is uncomfortable but not dangerous.


## And one warning

Downturns are prime season for scams, because fear sells better than greed: "recovery trades," "crash-proof" AI funds, guaranteed-return desks. A market decline doesn't create safe 30% opportunities — it creates *more* claims of them. The three structures of investment scams don't pause for red days; they recruit during them.

> You can't control whether this is a 2.6% wobble or the start of a -30% bear. You can control whether the automatic buys keep running, whether the cash buffer is real, and whether the allocation gets restored on schedule. Historically, that trio is what separated the people who came out of every Nasdaq drawdown wealthier from the people who didn't.
