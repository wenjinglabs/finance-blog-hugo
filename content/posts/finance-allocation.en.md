A widely cited finding in portfolio research (Brinson et al. and follow-ups) is that the large majority of a portfolio's return variability over time comes from **asset allocation policy** — how much sits in stocks vs bonds vs cash — not from which specific funds you picked or when you bought them. Yet the average investor spends ninety percent of their research time on the ten percent factor. This post is about the ninety.


## The three-bucket starting point



For the split between stable and growth, the old heuristic "100 minus your age" in the growth layer is crude but serviceable: ~70% growth-oriented at 30, ~50% at 50. The logic is what matters — **your capacity to sit through drawdowns declines as your horizon shortens**. Adjust for temperament: if a 30% drawdown would make you sell everything, your *behavioral* capacity is lower than the formula says.


## Rebalancing: the only scheduled action

Once the ratio is set, the strategy has exactly one recurring move: periodically restore the ratio. Worked example with a $20,000 growth portfolio at 60% stocks / 40% bonds:

- After a strong stock year: stocks grow to $13,200, bonds sit at $8,000. Total $21,200, now 62.3% / 37.7%.
- Rebalancing sells ~$480 of stocks and buys bonds, restoring 60/40. You have just **sold high and bought low** without having an opinion about where markets go next.


## The underrated half: psychology

A portfolio that is 100% equities is objectively fine on a 30-year spreadsheet and behaviorally fragile in real life, because the owner will experience several 30%+ drawdowns with no buffer and no plan. An allocated portfolio gives you two things at the worst moments: **money you don't have to sell** (the stable layers), and **a written rule for what to do** (rebalancing). Same market, same returns available — completely different odds you'll still be following the plan at the end.

> Allocation is less about maximizing returns on paper and more about maximizing the odds that you personally capture them.
