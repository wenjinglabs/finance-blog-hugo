Index funds and dollar-cost averaging get sold as a bundle, but they solve two different problems. An index fund solves **stock-picking**: you can't reliably pick winners, so you buy the whole market at near-zero cost. DCA solves **timing**: you can't reliably pick moments either, so you spread entries out. Understanding which problem each solves tells you when the advice stops applying.


## The fee math is the quiet superpower

Index funds' real edge is cost, and cost compounds just like returns. Here's the same investor — $10,000/year contributed, 7% gross annual return, 40 years:

- At 0.05% expense ratio (typical broad index fund): roughly **$2.0M**
- At 1.05% expense ratio (typical actively managed fund): roughly **$1.55M**

{{< chart type="hbar" title="Final wealth after 40 years: $10k/yr at 7% gross" labels="0.05% fee;1.05% fee" values="2000;1550" unit="k$" highlight="1" >}}

That 1% annual difference consumes about **23% of your final wealth** over 40 years. Fees are one of the very few variables in investing you fully control, which is why they're the first thing to check on any fund, before anything else about it.


## What DCA actually earns, and what it doesn't

- **It earns the market's long-run return** — the beta. Buying broad indexes is a bet that economies grow over decades. Historically, that bet has paid; it is not a guarantee for any particular market or period.
- **It smooths your entry price.** Fixed amounts buy more shares when prices are low. This only pays off if prices *recover* after falling. In a permanent decline, DCA just loses money more slowly.
- **It does not protect you from selling at the bottom.** DCA is a buying strategy. The damage in most real portfolios comes from the selling side — stopping the plan during a crash, which silently converts the recovery from your friend to a stranger.


## The three prerequisites

1. **Choose broad, not thematic.** Broad indexes (total market, S&P 500, developed+EM mixes) represent the average. Thematic funds are concentrated bets sold to you after they've already run up — the opposite of what made the index idea good.

> Index investing earns the market's return minus almost nothing. Most investors earn the market's return minus their own behavior. The gap between those two sentences is the entire game.
